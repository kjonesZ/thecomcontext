Communication
in Context.

Technical proficiency is essential. Graduates also need to connect their expertise to the people, systems, and decisions that depend on it.

The educational gap

Technical skill. A missing connection.

What technical education builds

  • Clean code
  • Network configurations
  • Working prototypes and control systems
  • Solutions that meet isolated specifications

Where the handoff breaks

  • Explaining tradeoffs to clinicians
  • Coordinating changes with operations teams
  • Connecting decisions to business needs
  • Navigating compliance with other teams

Students need practice translating technical expertise into shared decisions—especially where digital systems meet physical operations.

The cost of siloed learning

When the connection is missing.

Misaligned Implementations

A connected system meets the technical spec but misses the operating workflow. Teams inherit rework and new points of friction.

Stalled Rollouts

A rollout overlooks equipment constraints, training, or time needed for a safe change. Implementation stalls while teams resolve competing priorities.

Security Workarounds

A security procedure is unclear or does not fit daily operations. People may bypass it to keep work moving.

Illustrative risks—not inevitable outcomes or failures caused by learning alone.

Communication in Context responds to this gap by bringing those conversations into the learning experience—around shared problems with real people, constraints, and consequences.

A working definition

Communication in Context brings people from different disciplines into conversation around a shared problem, so students can connect technical knowledge with the setting in which it is used.

02 / Where it began

Experience gives knowledge a setting.

Faculty externships helped shape this idea: understanding a workplace means seeing how people work together, what decisions they face, and why a technical task matters. Kyle Jones’s NCyTE Faculty Externship annual reports document these connections across the 2023, 2024, and 2025 program years.

From workplace insight to classroom conversation.

The 2023 report describes plans for team-based capstones and collaboration with a speech department. The 2024 report highlights collaborative planning, documentation, and oral and written deliverables. In 2025, communication, governance, and structured decision-making emerge as recurring priorities.

Read the examples: 2023, pp. 8–10 · 2024, pp. 9–11 · 2025, pp. 8–10.

The IT/OT connection

When systems converge, expertise must connect.

The paper Bridging the IT/OT Cybersecurity Skills Gap places the educational gap in a concrete setting: information technology (IT) manages data and networks; operational technology (OT) monitors or controls physical processes, such as production equipment. As these systems connect, the paper argues that workforce preparation must connect their disciplines too.

Understand the other team’s context.

A network decision can also be an operations decision. Students need to ask what a change means for equipment, continuity, people, and the organization.

Make the decision understandable.

The paper identifies clear communication with technical and nontechnical audiences as a core competency. Explaining consequences belongs alongside configuring a system.

Paper: IT/OT convergence, pp. 7–8 · communication and curriculum, pp. 15–17.

Communication in Context carries these insights into a teaching question: how can students practice connecting their expertise with other people’s needs before entering the workplace? The shared problem, different perspectives, and decision brief below are a proposed classroom response.

03 / Guiding principles

Four ideas to build on.

01

Start with a shared problem.

Begin with a workplace need: connect equipment, support care, or improve a process. Let that purpose guide the technical work.

02

Bring different perspectives.

Bring IT, operations, business, and other relevant disciplines into the same decision. Ask what each team knows that the others need to understand.

03

Make the context visible.

Make equipment limits, workflows, security needs, costs, and human consequences part of the assignment—not background information.

04

Use conversation to connect.

Ask students to explain a recommendation to another role, clarify unfamiliar language, and document what changed after the conversation.

04 / In practice

A 30-minute cross-disciplinary seminar.

Use a shared workplace problem inside an existing course. The clinic example below explores how technical choices affect care and workflow; the manufacturing variation applies the IT/OT paper’s emphasis on communication across connected systems.

Try the IT/OT variation: connect a production machine

Prompt: A manufacturer wants production data from an existing machine to appear on a shared dashboard. What must the team understand before recommending how to connect it?

  • IT: What connections, data, and support will be needed?
  • Operations: How does the machine support production, and when can changes be considered?
  • Cybersecurity: What access and risk questions need investigation?
  • Business: What benefit justifies the cost and disruption?
  • Communication & design: Can operators and decision-makers understand the plan and their responsibilities?

Use the same 30-minute rhythm: assign roles, negotiate a tradeoff, and deliver one shared brief. Include the recommendation, an unresolved question, who must answer it, and one revision prompted by another role.

An illustrative classroom prompt informed by the paper’s competencies and curriculum discussion, pp. 13–17.

The 30-minute rhythm

Groups of five take the Technology, Healthcare, Business, Communication & Design, and Cybersecurity perspectives. Use the clinic prompt and matrix below; capture the decision in one shared document.

  1. Min 0–3

    Frame

    One shared problem statement.

  2. Min 3–8

    Pick Role

    One priority and one question per role.

  3. Min 8–16

    Negotiate

    One decision and its cross-disciplinary tradeoff.

  4. Min 16–23

    Draft Brief

    A recommendation, benefit, tradeoff, and revision.

  5. Min 23–30

    Exit Ticket

    One change in thinking after peer feedback.

Use assigned perspectives in a single-program class or disciplinary knowledge in a mixed group. Students label assumptions and questions for real stakeholders.

Classroom simulation prompt

A clinic wants to introduce a digital check-in system.

What would make the system useful, understandable, and appropriate for the people who rely on it?

Choose a perspective. Notice how the questions change.

  • TechnologyHow will the system connect, work reliably, and support its users?
  • HealthcareHow will it fit patient needs and the daily flow of care?
  • BusinessWhat resources, training, and process changes will it require?
  • Communication & designWill patients understand what to do, including when they need help?
  • CybersecurityWhat information is needed, who should access it, and how will it be protected?

Students compare these perspectives, identify tradeoffs, and explain a proposed approach together.

05 / From idea to learning

Begin with a small, shared experience.

A first experiment could pair an IT course with manufacturing, engineering, business, or healthcare around one challenge. Keep the technical learning goals, and add a shared explanation of the decision and its consequences.

The IT/OT paper also argues for industry partnerships and faculty externships to keep curriculum connected to workplace needs. Invite an employer or advisory-board member to help frame the problem, identify realistic constraints, and review the students’ recommendations (pp. 18–20).

Shared problem→Different perspectives→Shared understanding

Walk through a possible learning experience.

  1. Frame the situation. Describe the people, setting, and problem without prescribing the answer.
  2. Surface the questions. Let each discipline identify what it needs to understand.
  3. Develop an approach. Ask students to negotiate priorities and explain their choices.
  4. Reflect on the connections. Identify how another perspective changed the proposed solution.
  5. Use what emerges. Consider what the experience suggests for future assignments, partnerships, or program development.

07 / Questions to develop

Keep the concept open.

These questions can guide the next version of the framework.

What does “context” need to include?

People, purpose, workplace practices, disciplinary language, constraints, and consequences are starting points. For an IT/OT problem, include the physical process, equipment limitations, continuity needs, and the roles responsible for a change. What would a purely technical specification leave out?

What should students be able to do differently?

Possible outcomes include explaining a technical idea to another discipline, asking better questions, recognizing tradeoffs, and adapting a solution to its setting.

How will we recognize meaningful learning?

Look for evidence that students can explain how another perspective changed their thinking. Develop criteria that value both disciplinary knowledge and the connections between disciplines.

How can faculty externships inform the experience?

Explore how faculty can turn workplace observations into shared problems, teaching examples, and conversations with colleagues in other disciplines.

What is the first experiment worth trying?

Choose a manageable problem, a small group of collaborators, and one clear learning goal. Use the results to refine the concept before expanding it.
